## The optimal transport problem: history

The problem has two very different origins, separated by 160 years.

### Monge's earth-mover (1781)

Gaspard Monge posed the question in a military engineering context :Cite{bib="Monge1781"}: given a
terrain of excavated material (déblais) and a set of embankment sites (remblais) to fill,
find the **deterministic** assignment of soil parcels to sites that minimises the total
distance traveled. In modern notation, find a mapping `T : X → Y` such that `T` maps the
source distribution to exactly match the target distribution and minimises
`∫ c(x, T(x)) dμ(x)`. Monge's formulation is geometrically clean but algorithmically
rigid: it requires a bijection and lacks a standard convex relaxation (a smoothed version amenable to gradient-based or LP solvers), making it hard to
solve in general.

### Kantorovich's relaxation (1942)

Leonid Kantorovich proposed a different formulation :Cite{bib="Kantorovich1942"}: instead of
requiring a deterministic mapping, allow stochastic couplings — joint distributions `P` over
pairs `(x, y)` whose marginals match the source and target. A coupling is a joint
distribution `P(x, y)` whose marginals are the source and target — in discrete terms, a
matrix `P` where `P[i,j]` is the fraction of mass moved from source `i` to target `j`.
This turns the problem into a **linear program** (an optimization with a linear objective and linear constraints), which can be solved reliably and whose dual variables carry a useful economic interpretation. The optimal primal and dual objectives coincide exactly (LP strong duality), and the dual variables — one per source point, one per target point — can be read as the implicit "prices" that balance supply and demand, and the solution coincides
with Monge's optimal map under mild regularity conditions on the cost.

Kantorovich shared the 1975 Nobel Prize in Economics with Tjalling Koopmans for the
theory of optimal allocation of resources, with the transportation problem as a central
example. These dual variables (one scalar per source point, one per target point) reappear as the Sinkhorn potentials `f` and `g` described later in this page.

### The earth mover's distance in computer vision (1990s–2000s)

For most of the 20th century, optimal transport was studied as a mathematical object and as
a linear-programming special case. Rubner, Tomasi, and Guibas :Cite{bib="Rubner2000"} sharpened the
connection to computer vision and signal processing by introducing the **Earth Mover's
Distance (EMD)** as a practical similarity measure between histograms and distributions.
The EMD — equivalent to the 1-Wasserstein distance under ground metrics — is invariant to
cross-bin shifts, handles partial matches, and is perceptually meaningful for colour,
texture, and shape comparison. They also gave an efficient transportation-problem solver for
histograms. This work established OT as a tool a practitioner would actually reach for, not
just a mathematical construct.

### Villani and the mathematical renaissance (2003–2008)

Cédric Villani's two monographs :Cite{bib="Villani2003 Villani2008"} unified the geometry,
analysis, and probability theory of optimal transport and established it as a central object
in modern mathematics. Key results in this body of work — results such as the Brenier map (which shows when the optimal plan is a deterministic function) and displacement interpolation (geodesic paths between distributions) — gave optimal transport
a language that could speak to differential geometry, PDEs, and probability simultaneously.
Villani received the Fields Medal in 2010, partly for this work. Alongside this theoretical
consolidation, Benamou and Brenier :Cite{bib="BenamouBrenier2000"} gave a physical interpretation of the 2-Wasserstein distance `W_2` (the OT cost when using squared Euclidean distance): it equals the minimum kinetic energy required to continuously
deform one distribution into the other.

### Cuturi and the computational breakthrough (2013)

The exact transportation LP scales as `O((n + m)^3)` via network simplex — tractable for
small histograms (n, m ≤ a few hundred) but far too slow for the million-point batches
common in deep learning. Marco Cuturi's NeurIPS 2013 paper :Cite{bib="Cuturi2013"} changed this
entirely. By adding an entropy term to the objective, the regularised problem has a unique
solution that satisfies **Sinkhorn's matrix-scaling conditions** :Cite{bib="Sinkhorn1967"}: the
optimal coupling factors as `P_ε = diag(u) K diag(v)` where `K_ij = exp(−c_ij / ε)`. The
Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration — alternating row and column normalizations — converges to this
solution in `O(n^2)` per iteration with simple element-wise and matrix-vector operations (BLAS level-1) that execute efficiently on modern GPU architectures. The trade-off is a regularisation bias that smears the plan; the
bias shrinks with `ε` but convergence slows.

This paper opened the door to large-scale OT in machine learning: within a year, OT losses
appeared in generative models, sequence alignment, and distribution comparison tasks. It is
the direct ancestor of the `LOG_SINKHORN` backend in torchmatch.

### The ML explosion (2013–present)

From 2013 onward, OT moved rapidly from applied mathematics into the ML mainstream:

- **Wasserstein GAN** :Cite{bib="Arjovsky2017"}: OT as a training objective for generative adversarial
  networks. The Wasserstein distance gives a meaningful gradient even when the generated and
  real distributions have disjoint support, where Jensen-Shannon divergence saturates.
- **Sinkhorn divergence** :Cite{bib="Genevay2018"}: debiasing the regularised loss to recover a true
  divergence that is positive, symmetric, and zero only when the two distributions match.
- **Unbalanced OT** :Cite{bib="Chizat2018 Sejourne2019"}: KL-relaxed marginals for distributions
  with different total mass, enabling robust matching in the presence of outliers.
- **Point-cloud OT at scale**: streaming Triton kernels (the `samples.loss` backend in
  torchmatch) that avoid materialising the full cost matrix by computing costs and
  log-sum-exp in blocks within GPU registers.

## The optimal transport problem

Given two probability distributions `a` over `n` source points and `b` over `m` target
points, and a cost matrix `C` with `c_ij = cost(x_i, y_j)`, the **Kantorovich formulation**
:Cite{bib="Kantorovich1942"} of optimal transport seeks a coupling (joint distribution) `P` with
marginals `a` and `b` that minimises the total transport cost:

```
OT(a, b, C) = min_{P ∈ U(a, b)} ⟨P, C⟩
```

where `U(a, b)` is the set of all `n × m` matrices with non-negative entries and row sums
`a`, column sums `b`. When `a` and `b` are uniform (as in the torchmatch defaults), the
coupling is a doubly-stochastic matrix. The optimal plan `P*` concentrates on as few cells
as possible; for smooth costs it has at most `n + m − 1` non-zero entries.

For the **continuous** (samples-face) case, the points `x_i ∈ ℝ^d` and `y_j ∈ ℝ^d` are
given directly and the cost is the squared Euclidean distance `c_ij = |x_i − y_j|^2`. The
2-Wasserstein distance `W_2^2(a, b) = OT(a, b, C)` is then a metric on the space of
probability measures and has clean geometric properties for gradient-based optimisation.

## Entropic regularisation and Sinkhorn

The unregularised LP is non-differentiable with respect to the cost and produces a sparse
plan. **Entropic regularisation** :Cite{bib="Cuturi2013"} adds a KL penalty:

```
OT_ε(a, b, C) = min_{P ∈ U(a, b)} ⟨P, C⟩ − ε H(P)
```

where `H(P) = −∑_ij P_ij log P_ij` is the entropy of the coupling. The regularised plan
`P_ε` is the unique positive matrix satisfying the Schrödinger bridge conditions (the
fixed-point conditions that characterise the entropic OT solution: the plan factors as a
product of two diagonal scalings applied to the Gibbs kernel):

```
P_ε = diag(u) · K · diag(v)       K_ij = exp(−c_ij / ε)
```

for some positive vectors `u` and `v` that enforce the marginals. **Sinkhorn's algorithm**
:Cite{bib="Sinkhorn1967"} iterates:

```
u ← a / (K v)
v ← b / (K^T u)
```

until convergence. Each iteration is an element-wise division followed by a matrix-vector
product, and the algorithm converges linearly with rate `1 − 1/n` in the worst case.

### Log-domain stabilisation

For small `ε` or large costs, `K_ij = exp(−c_ij / ε)` underflows to zero. torchmatch
uses the log-domain (Gibbs-potential) formulation :Cite{bib="Schmitzer2019"}:

```
f_i = ε log u_i,   g_j = ε log v_j
f ← ε log a − ε LSE_j((g_j − c_ij) / ε)
g ← ε log b − ε LSE_i((f_i − c_ij) / ε)
```

where `f_i` and `g_j` are the Sinkhorn potentials — the log-domain representations of the
dual variables `u` and `v` — and `LSE` is log-sum-exp. This is numerically stable for all
`ε > 0` and all cost magnitudes. The log-plan returned by `torchmatch.transport.matrix.solve` is
`log_plan_ij = (f_i + g_j − c_ij) / ε`; exponentiate to recover `P_ε`.

## Sinkhorn divergence (debiasing)

The raw Sinkhorn loss `OT_ε(a, b, C)` is not a proper divergence: it is not symmetric and
does not equal zero when `a = b`. Both defects come from the self-transport bias introduced
by the entropy term — the regularisation makes every distribution slightly non-zero mass at
every point, so `OT_ε(a, a, C) > 0` even when the source and target are identical; this
bias is subtracted out by the debiasing step. **Sinkhorn divergence** :Cite{bib="Genevay2018"}
corrects this:

```
SD_ε(a, b) = OT_ε(a, b, C) − ½ OT_ε(a, a, C_aa) − ½ OT_ε(b, b, C_bb)
```

where `C_aa` and `C_bb` are the cost matrices computed between source–source and
target–target point pairs. `SD_ε(a, b) ≥ 0` with equality iff `a = b`; it is symmetric;
and as `ε → 0` it converges to the exact unregularised OT cost (and is related to the Maximum Mean Discrepancy, a kernel-based distribution distance :Cite{bib="Sejdinovic2013"}).

torchmatch computes `SD_ε` by running three Sinkhorn iterations (one cross, two self) and
combining. The `samples.loss` face handles all three calls internally under `debias=True`;
the `matrix.solve` face does so under `backend=SINKHORN_DIVERGENCE`.

## Unbalanced optimal transport

Standard OT forces the coupling to exactly match the source marginal `a` and target
marginal `b`. When the distributions have **different total mass** (outliers, partial
overlap, domain shift), this constraint corrupts the plan by forcing every source point to
send mass somewhere. **Unbalanced OT** :Cite{bib="Chizat2018"} replaces the hard marginal
constraint with a KL divergence penalty:

```
UOT_ε(a, b, C) = min_{P ≥ 0} ⟨P, C⟩ − ε H(P)
                 + ρ KL(P 1 | a) + ρ KL(P^T 1 | b)
```

The parameter `ρ` (called `rho` in the API, and related to `reach` via `rho = reach^2`)
controls the trade-off: large `ρ` recovers balanced OT; small `ρ` allows large mass
deviations. The algorithm is a **scaling iteration** analogous to balanced Sinkhorn but
with a softer marginal update:

```
u ← (a / (K v))^{ρ / (ρ + ε)}
v ← (b / (K^T u))^{ρ / (ρ + ε)}
```

The exponent `ρ / (ρ + ε)` is the "softening factor"; it equals 1 (exact marginals) when
`ρ → ∞` and 0 (no marginal constraint) when `ρ → 0`.

torchmatch implements unbalanced OT in the log domain under `UNBALANCED_SINKHORN` and the
`reach` / `reach_x` / `reach_y` kwargs of `samples.loss`.

## Network simplex (exact EMD)

When the exact optimal plan is required, the entropic shortcut is unavailable. torchmatch
exposes a **network simplex** solver :Cite{bib="Orlin1997"} under `EXACT_EMD`. The network simplex is a specialized algorithm for minimum-cost flow problems (a class of LP closely related to transport) that runs much faster than a generic LP solver by exploiting the fact that optimal transport solutions always have a tree-structured support. For the transport polytope it runs in `O((n + m)^3 log(n + m))` worst-case
time and is typically much faster in practice.

`EXACT_EMD` is CPU-only, not differentiable (it returns a sparse plan whose argmax is
discontinuous), and intended for small problems where exact solutions are needed for ground
truth or comparison.

## Matrix-face implementation

The four `transport.matrix` backends are implemented as **Python custom ops** registered
with `torch.library` against the `transport` namespace. They share a common input contract
(validated by the `_solve.py` dispatcher) and diverge only in the inner loop.

### Python LSE loops on CPU and CUDA

`LOG_SINKHORN`, `SINKHORN_DIVERGENCE`, and `UNBALANCED_SINKHORN` are all implemented as
plain PyTorch tensor operations — no handwritten CUDA kernels. The log-domain update

```
f ← ε log a − ε LSE_j((g_j − c_ij) / ε)
g ← ε log b − ε LSE_i((f_i − c_ij) / ε)
```

translates directly to `torch.logsumexp` calls over the cost tensor. Because
`torch.logsumexp` dispatches to cuBLAS/cuDNN on CUDA and to BLAS on CPU, both devices are
supported without separate kernel code. The per-iteration cost is one `logsumexp` across
rows and one across columns of the `(N, M)` (or `(B, N, M)`) cost tensor.

This means:
- The cost matrix is **fully materialised** in memory for every iteration.
- Memory footprint is `O(B × N × M)` for the cost plus `O(B × (N + M))` for the
  Sinkhorn potentials `f` and `g`.
- Gradients flow through `torch.logsumexp` automatically via standard PyTorch autograd —
  no `register_autograd` needed.
- `torch.compile` traces the loop cleanly: each `logsumexp` is an op in the graph.

### Exact EMD: C++ network simplex

`EXACT_EMD` is the only backend backed by a compiled extension. The network simplex
solver lives in `sources/torchmatch/transport/matrix/cpu/exact/` and is registered as a
C++ custom op (`ops.cpp` + `exact_emd_op.cpp`). A CUDA fragment header declares the
`transport` namespace but provides no CUDA implementation for EMD, so the op is CPU-only.
The C++ extension is built into `_transport_cpu_impl.so`; if the prebuilt `.so` is absent,
`load_cpu()` JIT-compiles it on first import.

### Dispatcher and FakeTensor shapes

The `_solve.py` dispatcher validates inputs — dtype (must be float), ndim (2 or 3),
presence of NaN / −inf — then selects a backend and calls the corresponding custom op.
All four matrix-face ops are registered with **FakeTensor kernels** in `_loader.py` — lightweight shape-only implementations that let `torch.compile` and `torch.export` infer output shapes without running the real computation:

- `register_batch_plan_fakes`: `(B, N, M) → (B, N, M)` for `LOG_SINKHORN` and
  `UNBALANCED_SINKHORN`.
- `register_divergence_fakes`: `(B, N, M) → (B,)` for `SINKHORN_DIVERGENCE`.
- The EMD shape matches its input.

These allow `torch.compile` and `torch.export` to trace through `matrix.solve` without
executing the real kernels.

## Samples-face implementation

The `transport.samples` face uses a fundamentally different strategy: the squared-Euclidean
cost `c_ij = |x_i − y_j|^2` is **never stored as an `N × M` matrix**. Instead, a Triton
streaming kernel computes costs block-by-block and fuses each block's contribution into the
Sinkhorn log-sum-exp accumulators without writing the full cost to memory.

### Streaming Sinkhorn in Triton

The kernel divides the source point set and the target point set into tiles. For each
`(source_tile, target_tile)` pair, a Triton program block:

1. Loads the tile of source coordinates and the tile of target coordinates.
2. Computes the `tile_size × tile_size` block of squared-Euclidean costs in registers.
3. Accumulates the per-source-point log-sum-exp update into the target Sinkhorn potential
   `g`, and the per-target-point update into the source potential `f`, without writing the
   cost block to global memory.

After one pass through all tile pairs, the potentials `f` (length `N`) and `g` (length
`M`) reflect one full Sinkhorn iteration. Multiple passes complete the iteration loop. The
memory footprint is `O(N + M)` for the potentials — independent of the cost matrix size.

This approach is analogous to FlashAttention's memory-efficient attention computation and
enables point-cloud sizes that would exceed GPU memory if the full cost matrix were
materialised. At `N = M = 65 536` on an A100 the full float32 cost matrix would require
16 GB; the streaming kernel runs in a few hundred megabytes of potential storage.

### CUDA-only constraint

Triton requires a CUDA device. Calling `transport.samples.loss` with CPU tensors raises a
`RuntimeError` immediately at the entry point; no CPU fallback is provided. Users who need
OT between point clouds on CPU should compute the cost matrix explicitly and use
`transport.matrix.solve`.

### Autograd

The forward pass (`_sinkhorn_samples_fwd`) is registered as a `torch.library.custom_op`.
The backward pass is registered separately via `torch.library.register_autograd` in
`_autograd.py`, providing analytic gradients with respect to both `x` and `y`. This means:

- The backward does not store the full `N × M` cost or plan — it recomputes the necessary
  quantities from the stored potentials `f` and `g`.
- Both `x.requires_grad` and `y.requires_grad` are supported independently.
- `torch.compile` captures the forward and backward together in a single graph.

### Supported modes

All modes (`debias`, `reach`, `reach_x`, `reach_y`) are implemented within the streaming
kernel framework. `debias=True` runs three forward passes (cross, source-self,
target-self) and combines the potentials to compute the Sinkhorn divergence; it does not
materialise the self-cost matrices.

## Comparing the backends

| Property | `LOG_SINKHORN` | `SINKHORN_DIVERGENCE` | `UNBALANCED_SINKHORN` | `EXACT_EMD` |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Output | log-plan | scalar | log-plan | dense plan |
| Differentiable | yes | yes | yes | no |
| Symmetric | no | yes | no | yes |
| Zero at `a=b` | no | yes | no | yes |
| Marginal constraints | exact | exact (debiased) | KL-relaxed | exact |
| Hardware | CPU + CUDA | CPU + CUDA | CPU + CUDA | CPU only |
| Complexity | `O(NM · n_iter)` | `3×` LOG_SINKHORN | `O(NM · n_iter)` | `O((N+M)^3 log)` |
